# Story: Two Managers Disagreeing on Priorities (Moderna Knowledge Base)

Practice date: 2026-11-20. Prompt: "Two managers on your team disagree on priorities and the disagreement is slowing the org down. How do you resolve it without taking every decision yourself?"

## The story in Shridhar's words (facts)

At Moderna, Shridhar is building a company knowledge base. Knowledge gets ingested two ways: unstructured data in Word documents, PDFs, and SharePoint, and structured data stored in databases. Two projects sit under that work. One builds the knowledge base itself by ingesting unstructured documents. The other builds a platform that converts unstructured documents into structured documents anyone can use outside the knowledge base.

Two managers on his team disagreed on priorities, and the disagreement was slowing both projects down. The manager who owned the knowledge base wanted depth: take one set of documents and build the full experience before expanding. The manager who owned the platform wanted breadth: cover more document types first so the platform could serve everyone.

Shridhar did not decide for them. His first ask was that each manager independently write down the business priorities along with the technical pros and cons of their approach, then come into a room and work through the impact of slowing down one project versus the other, judged against the bigger organizational goal.

The deciding factor was business feedback. The business needed to see the functionality working across multiple sets of documents before they could say what mattered. Depth first would have given them one polished slice and no sense of the whole. The decision: breadth first up to a point, then select one area to go deep, in that order. They prioritized the Veeva knowledge base across document sets, and then moved to the platform solution.

The durable fix mattered more than the decision. Shridhar told both managers that next time, they come back together, not separately. They work through the tradeoff on a whiteboard, they bring a joint recommendation with the pros and cons, and if they still cannot agree, they bring a joint escalation. His job is to weigh in on the recommendation, not to be the tiebreaker for every priority call.

## What went well in the mock

- A real, current example with a concrete mechanism: independent written pros and cons, then a joint session that weighed the cost of slowing each project. The mechanism is what this question tests, and it was specific rather than generic.
- The decision was tied to the organizational goal, business feedback across document sets, rather than to personal preference or the louder voice.
- The close is the strongest part: the framework where the two managers return with a joint recommendation next time. That is exactly what "without taking every decision yourself" means.

## What went wrong in the mock

- The opening ran long in the abstract before reaching the example. The framework-first preamble ate time that belonged to the story. Lead with the example, then pull the principle out of it.
- Breadth versus depth got tangled mid-answer. He corrected himself partway through, and by the end it was unclear which manager wanted which. In the room, name each position in one sentence each before going further.
- The result was missing in the first telling. The decision and what happened after only surfaced under probing. The outcome, breadth first, then depth in one area, belongs in the main answer, not in a follow-up.

## The ideal answer (~2 minutes spoken)

"At Moderna I had two managers on my team disagreeing on priorities, and the disagreement was slowing both projects down. One owned the knowledge base and wanted to go deep: take one set of documents and build the full experience before expanding. The other owned the platform that converts unstructured documents into structured ones, and wanted breadth: cover more document types first so the platform could serve everyone.

I did not decide for them. I asked each of them to write down, independently, the business priorities and the technical pros and cons of their approach, and then we sat in a room and worked through one question: what is the cost of slowing down each project, and which one matters more to the organization right now.

The deciding factor was business feedback. The business needed to see the functionality working across multiple sets of documents before they could tell us what mattered. Depth first would have given them one polished slice and no sense of the whole. So we decided on breadth first, up to a point, and then we would pick one area to go deep, in that order. We prioritized the Veeva knowledge base across document sets, and then moved to the platform solution.

The part I care about more than the decision is what we changed afterward. I told them that next time, they come back together, not separately. They work through the tradeoff on a whiteboard, they bring me a joint recommendation with the pros and cons, and if they still cannot agree, they bring a joint escalation. My job is to weigh in on the recommendation, not to be the tiebreaker for every priority call. That is how the org stops slowing down every time two managers disagree."

## Likely follow-ups, with the honest answers

- "What if they had come back still disagreeing?" : "Then they bring me a joint escalation: both positions, the pros and cons, and their recommendation. I decide, but the framing is theirs, and the decision is recorded with the reasoning so the next disagreement starts from it."
- "Why breadth first? Was depth wrong?" : "Depth was not wrong, it was a sequencing call. The business needed to see the functionality across document sets to give useful feedback. Once we had that feedback, depth in one area was the next step, not a rejected idea."
- "How do you know the framework stuck?" : "The test is the next disagreement. If it arrives as a joint recommendation with pros and cons, the framework worked. If it arrives as two separate complaints, I send them back to the whiteboard."
